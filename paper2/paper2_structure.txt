Resource-Aware Cooperative World Models at the Edge1. Introduction and Core ThesisThe evolution of connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs) has initiated a paradigm shift from isolated, ego-centric perception to multi-agent cooperative driving architectures. By sharing sensor data, intermediate feature representations, or object-level tracks across vehicular networks, CAVs can transcend fundamental physical limitations such as severe occlusion, limited sensor range, and adverse environmental conditions. Recent advances in cooperative motion prediction (CMP) have demonstrated that aggregating cooperative perception and prediction data significantly mitigates propagated uncertainties, resulting in superior tracking and forecasting performance compared to single-agent systems.1However, the deployment of state-of-the-art (SOTA) cooperative world models faces severe infrastructure and computational bottlenecks when scaled to dense traffic scenarios involving dozens of interacting agents. The prevailing assumption in many cooperative driving frameworks—particularly those relying on direct Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) communication—is the continuous availability of ideal, high-bandwidth, and low-latency wireless networks. Frameworks like CMP are typically trained offline, optimizing for raw perceptual accuracy and prediction precision without accounting for realistic networking constraints such as packet loss, accumulative jitter, and asynchronous data arrival.3 Under dense participation (e.g., 25 to 50+ agents), V2V networks suffer from catastrophic channel congestion and packet collisions, rendering the exchange of high-dimensional intermediate feature maps unviable.5 Consequently, both academia and industry are pivoting toward centralized, edge-hosted architectures utilizing Vehicle-to-Infrastructure-to-Vehicle (V2I/V2N2V) topologies supported by Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) and advanced 5G/6G infrastructure.7While centralized edge computing provides the necessary channel bandwidth and aggregated computational authority, it introduces a novel and equally critical failure mode: the dense-scale deadline cliff. The core thesis of this investigation establishes that a centralized, edge-hosted cooperative world-model pipeline—built from intermediate fusion, learned state space model (SSM) tracking, and multimodal prediction—encounters a severe, non-linear deadline cliff under bounded compute capacity. As the number of participating agents grows, cross-stage queueing and computational pressure escalate quadratically, pushing the publish-time and use-time state estimates beyond the strict real-time deadlines required by autonomous planning modules.To address this computationally induced failure boundary, a runtime resource-aware controller must be integrated directly into the edge orchestration layer. By dynamically and jointly adapting the service update rate, the model fidelity (such as voxel resolution and prediction horizons), and the architectural model tier based on available edge compute and instantaneous queue depths, this controller successfully shifts the deadline cliff to the right. Crucially, the optimization objective relies not on generic perception metrics like mean Average Precision (mAP), but on preserving planner-relevant utility. This ensures that the most safety-critical state outputs are delivered within a planner-feasible latency region, ultimately improving closed-loop success and minimizing intervention rates under heavy systemic load.9This research distinctively advances the domain by moving beyond earlier correctness limits established for deliberately simple stacks (e.g., late fusion paired with standard AB3DMOT and linear prediction), which primarily focused on state inconsistency and self-ghosting. Instead, this study targets a richer, highly realistic edge workload to characterize the deadline cliff under dense participation of up to 64 agents, defining deadline-qualified utility at the planner boundary, and implementing a multi-variable runtime adaptation controller tailored for the Symposium on Edge Computing (SEC) contexts.112. Exhaustive Review of Related Work and Subsystem EvolutionThe necessity of an adaptive, edge-hosted world model can only be fully understood by analyzing the simultaneous evolution of cooperative perception paradigms, multi-object tracking architectures, and the networking topologies designed to support them.2.1 The Evolution of Cooperative Perception and Motion PredictionThe trajectory of cooperative perception has been defined by the search for an optimal trade-off between communication bandwidth and perceptual fidelity. Early fusion architectures require vehicles to transmit raw sensor data, such as unprocessed LiDAR point clouds or high-resolution RGB video feeds, to nearby agents or infrastructure. While this approach theoretically preserves the maximum amount of contextual information and allows for optimal downstream processing, it demands prohibitive network bandwidth—often exceeding gigabits per second for a single intersection—making it structurally unscalable for dense traffic environments.12Conversely, late fusion architectures require each vehicle to independently process its raw sensor data into object-level tracks (e.g., 3D bounding boxes and velocity vectors). These highly compressed state vectors are then broadcasted to the network. While late fusion is exceptionally bandwidth-efficient, it suffers from severe performance degradation when local detections are noisy, uncertain, or plagued by occlusion. If a vehicle's local sensor cannot confidently detect an object, no information about that spatial region is shared, defeating the primary purpose of cooperation.12Intermediate fusion has emerged as the dominant and most effective paradigm. In this architecture, raw sensor data is processed by a local neural network encoder to extract intermediate, deep-learned feature maps—typically projected into a Bird's Eye View (BEV) representation. These intermediate maps are spatially compressed and transmitted. A receiving node (or edge server) then merges these feature maps using spatial-temporal attention mechanisms, allowing the network to dynamically weigh features based on learned semantic value rather than rigid hand-crafted rules.12Building upon the intermediate fusion paradigm, state-of-the-art frameworks like Cooperative Motion Prediction (CMP) have unified perception and trajectory forecasting into a single end-to-end differentiable pipeline.1 By aggregating intermediate features before the tracking and prediction stages, CMP effectively mitigates the cascading propagation of upstream detection inaccuracies. Studies demonstrate that CMP reduces average prediction errors by 12.3% to 17.2% compared to non-cooperative baselines, while simultaneously reducing the rate of missing detections in highly occluded environments.1 However, a critical gap remains: frameworks like CMP are heavily optimized through offline training using idealized synthetic datasets (e.g., OPV2V) or curated, low-density real-world datasets (e.g., V2V4Real).2 This offline training relies on the faulty assumption that multi-agent feature maps will arrive synchronously, without packet loss, and with deterministic, ultra-low latency.32.2 Advancements in Multi-Object Tracking via State Space ModelsRobust Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) serves as the critical bridge between instantaneous spatial perception and temporal trajectory forecasting. Historically, MOT architectures relied heavily on variants of the Kalman Filter (KF) to propagate bounding box states over time. While computationally lightweight, KF-based trackers assume linear, Gaussian motion models, which fail severely when tracking objects that exhibit highly non-linear, unpredictable, or diverse motion dynamics typical of dense urban driving.17To capture non-linear dynamics, the field pivoted toward deep learning models, initially utilizing Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and more recently, self-attention-based Transformers. While Transformers achieved unprecedented accuracy by modeling long-range global dependencies across time, their computational complexity scales quadratically ($O(L^2)$) with the sequence length $L$. In a dense cooperative scenario tracking hundreds of objects over long temporal horizons, Transformer-based MOT quickly depletes available GPU memory and violates real-time processing constraints.18Recently, structured State Space Models (SSMs), particularly the Mamba architecture, have revolutionized sequence modeling by rivaling Transformer accuracy while maintaining linear computational complexity ($O(L)$).20 The underlying mathematics of an SSM defines a continuous-time state space where a hidden state $h(t)$ captures the motion history, and $x(t)$ represents the incoming spatial features:$$\dot{h}(t) = Ah(t) + Bx(t)$$$$y(t) = Ch(t) + Dx(t)$$Through hardware-aware discretization—such as employing a zero-order hold with a timescale parameter $\Delta$—the continuous parameters $A$ and $B$ are transformed into discrete matrices $\bar{A}$ and $\bar{B}$. This discretization allows the model to process sequences recurrently during rapid inference while training efficiently in parallel.18Architectures like TrackSSM leverage these discrete Mamba blocks to build highly efficient motion encoders.18 By utilizing a Flow-SSM module to calculate positional gradients and guide temporal state transitions, SSM-based trackers can maintain accurate bounding box representations through extended periods of occlusion, which are common in dense traffic or during transient network failures.17 In the context of cooperative edge pipelines, integrating SSMs with strict State-Based Association (SBA) is paramount. SBA algorithms ensure rigorous bipartite matching, preventing identical physical entities viewed from different vehicle angles from spawning duplicate tracks. Enforcing SBA throughout the pipeline meticulously removes identity-confusion confounds, guaranteeing that any observed performance degradation under load is strictly attributable to computational deadline failures rather than algorithmic matching errors.222.3 Edge Computing and Resource-Aware OrchestrationThe transition from decentralized V2V processing to centralized Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) fundamentally alters the operational constraints of the autonomous driving pipeline. Edge computing bridges the gap between the ultra-low latency requirements of vehicle control and the massive computational power typically reserved for remote cloud data centers. By deploying edge servers at roadside units (RSUs) or 5G gNodeB base stations, computation is brought directly to the network's periphery.24While MEC eliminates the V2V channel bandwidth crisis, it is constrained by rigid hardware budgets, strict thermal envelopes, and limited power availability.10 Traditional real-time operating systems (RTOS) like QNX or platforms built on the AUTOSAR Adaptive standard excel at deterministic scheduling for static workloads but struggle to manage the extreme variance introduced by dynamic, multi-agent neural network inference.27To prevent systemic failure, modern edge deployments require resource-aware service orchestration. Prior literature has explored adaptive offloading, where tasks are dynamically routed between the vehicle, edge, and cloud based on network telemetry, as well as dynamic neural networks that utilize early-exit branches or static channel pruning.28 However, a critical gap exists in the literature regarding unified, multi-variable controllers designed explicitly for cooperative world models. Existing systems rarely execute simultaneous optimization of service rate, architectural fidelity, and model tiering driven by a planner-specific utility objective.3. The V2V Networking Fallacy: A Comprehensive StudyA foundational claim of this report is that direct Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) communication architectures are fundamentally incapable of supporting dense-scale intermediate fusion pipelines. To justify the migration to a centralized edge-hosted framework, a rigorous analysis of V2V bandwidth, latency, and jitter constraints is required.3.1 Bandwidth Saturation and Channel CongestionCurrent V2V topologies rely on two primary Radio Access Technologies (RATs): Dedicated Short-Range Communication (DSRC), based on the IEEE 802.11p standard, and Cellular V2X (C-V2X), utilizing the 3GPP Release 14/15 PC5 sidelink interface.7 Both protocols exhibit fatal scaling flaws when confronted with the immense payload requirements of sharing intermediate feature tensors across 25 to 50+ interacting agents.In an intermediate fusion model, each vehicle must process raw sensor data into a compressed BEV feature map. Even with aggressive spatial compression techniques, a high-fidelity feature tensor typically represents a payload of approximately 200 KB per frame. To maintain a safe autonomous control loop, the system demands an update rate of at least 10 Hz. Consequently, a single vehicle requires a continuous dedicated throughput of 16 Mbps. In a dense urban intersection scenario involving 40 participating vehicles, the aggregate channel demand reaches a staggering 640 Mbps.This demand vastly exceeds physical layer capabilities. DSRC operates in the 5.9 GHz ITS band and offers a maximum theoretical throughput of merely 27 Mbps under pristine, idealized laboratory conditions.8 C-V2X PC5, while offering superior spectral efficiency and range, generally plateaus near 50 Mbps in standard operational configurations.6 Therefore, supporting 40 agents sharing intermediate features requires over an order of magnitude more bandwidth than the physical channel can provide.3.2 MAC Layer Failures: CSMA/CA and SPS CollapseThe bandwidth deficit is further compounded by the catastrophic collapse of Medium Access Control (MAC) layer protocols under dense load. IEEE 802.11p relies on Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA). As the number of transmitting agents increases, the Channel Busy Ratio (CBR) approaches critical thresholds (typically &gt;80%). In this saturated state, vehicles continually sense a busy channel and trigger exponential backoff algorithms. This leads to massive, unpredictable transmission delays and a precipitous collapse in the Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR), often dropping below 30% in high-density simulations.7Conversely, C-V2X employs Semi-Persistent Scheduling (SPS) to allocate resource blocks. While SPS is significantly more deterministic than CSMA/CA in moderate traffic, it suffers from inherent half-duplex limitations and inevitable resource block collisions when vehicular density exceeds the pool of available sub-channels.6 When collisions occur, consecutive packets are systematically dropped, leading to track fragmentation in the downstream perception modules.3.3 Accumulative Jitter and the Synchronization PenaltyBeyond raw throughput and packet loss, intermediate fusion frameworks like CMP are uniquely vulnerable to accumulative jitter. Cooperative world models require that feature maps from distinct physical viewpoints be tightly synchronized in both space and time to form a coherent global representation.In a multi-hop V2V network, routing delays and dynamic topology changes induce severe jitter. If Vehicle A's feature map arrives at $t + 5 \text{ ms}$ and Vehicle B's feature map arrives at $t + 45 \text{ ms}$, the receiving vehicle's fusion module faces a critical dilemma. It must either buffer Vehicle A's data while waiting for Vehicle B—thereby increasing the overall Age of Information (AoI) of the entire fused tensor—or it must proceed immediately, discarding Vehicle B's contribution and severely degrading the spatial completeness of the perception output.31This synchronization penalty completely dismantles the core premise of offline-trained CMP models, which inherently assume synchronous, uniformly available data.13 The analytical conclusion is incontrovertible: decentralized V2V cannot support next-generation cooperative world models.The shift to a V2I/V2N2V paradigm leveraging 5G and MEC infrastructure resolves the bandwidth and collision crises. Cellular infrastructure provides high-capacity uplinks utilizing orthogonal frequency-division multiple access (OFDMA), drastically reducing interference.7 More importantly, it centralizes the temporal synchronization of incoming feature maps at a highly provisioned edge node, enabling the execution of complex world models. However, while solving the network bottleneck, this architecture fundamentally shifts the point of failure to the computational capacity of the edge server itself.4. System Stack Architecture: The Edge-Hosted Cooperative World ModelTo rigorously evaluate the limits of centralized execution, it is necessary to define a comprehensive, structurally modern SOTA system stack. The architecture under investigation deliberately moves beyond simplistic models—such as basic late fusion paired with linear prediction algorithms—that artificially highlight state inconsistency. Instead, this system executes a richer, compute-heavy workload characterized by intermediate fusion, continuous SSM tracking, and deep multimodal prediction, accurately reflecting the processing demands of a true 2026-era edge deployment.4.1 Intermediate Fusion Perception StageThe perception pipeline initiates asynchronously on the local compute units of each participating CAV. High-resolution raw sensor data, primarily generated by multi-return LiDAR arrays and high-framerate camera clusters, is processed by a lightweight, hardware-accelerated local encoder. This encoder—often a variant of PointPillars or a structurally pruned ResNet backbone—projects the unstructured 3D point clouds and 2D images into a dense, highly semantic Bird's Eye View (BEV) feature map.14These localized BEV feature maps are aggressively quantized and transmitted via reliable 5G Uu uplinks to the central edge server. Upon arrival, the edge server's intermediate fusion module must execute complex spatial alignment algorithms to project all $N$ incoming maps into a unified global coordinate frame. Following alignment, the fusion mechanism utilizes a sophisticated multi-head spatial-temporal attention mechanism to dynamically merge the maps.12This attention mechanism is the first major source of compute pressure. Rather than employing static averaging rules, the attention matrices dynamically calculate the cross-covariance between overlapping features, assigning higher weights to features originating from viewpoints with high visibility and low uncertainty. The mathematical complexity of global cross-attention scales quadratically, $O(N^2 \cdot P)$, where $N$ is the number of participating agents and $P$ is the spatial resolution of the feature grid. As agent density increases, the matrix multiplication requirements rapidly saturate the edge server's tensor cores.4.2 SSM-Based Multi-Object TrackerFollowing the generation of a cohesive global BEV feature map and subsequent bounding box extraction, the pipeline transitions to temporal tracking. The edge server employs a robust SSM-based multi-object tracker to associate detections across temporal frames.18This tracking module utilizes a Mamba-Block motion encoder tailored specifically for processing highly variable historical trajectory data. The core innovation lies in the Flow-SSM module, which calculates the exact positional and kinematic gradients between consecutive frames to guide the continuous temporal state transitions of all active bounding boxes.18 By leveraging data-dependent structured state spaces, the tracker maintains an internal memory of object dynamics that proves highly resilient to transient occlusions or dropped Uu packets.17As established earlier, this pipeline mandates the strict usage of State-Based Association (SBA). SBA ensures that the cost matrix used for bipartite matching (e.g., via the Hungarian algorithm) heavily penalizes physically impossible track bifurcations, effectively forcing the system to collapse duplicate views of the same vehicle into a singular, cohesive identity.34 By guaranteeing algorithmic identity consistency through SBA, any failure observed during dense participation can be definitively attributed to computational deadline misses rather than algorithmic matching flaws.4.3 Multimodal Trajectory PredictorThe final stage of the cooperative world model is the multimodal trajectory predictor. Operating on the clean, unified tracks provided by the SSM tracker, this deep learning module is tasked with forecasting $K$ distinct, physically plausible future trajectories for all tracked entities over a forward horizon of $T_h$ seconds.The predictor leverages an advanced Transformer-based decoding architecture to model the intricate web of spatiotemporal relationships.1 This involves calculating the temporal dynamics of individual agents, modeling the complex spatial interactions and potential collision vectors between all adjacent agents, and constraining these predicted paths using vectorized High-Definition (HD) map topologies. The output is a highly structured tensor containing $K$ predicted paths per agent, each accompanied by an estimated probability distribution.16 Evaluating the mutual interactions and calculating conditional probabilities for dozens of rapidly moving agents requires massive parallel computation, forming the secondary compute bottleneck in the pipeline.4.4 Centralized Edge Execution and Planner-Aware UtilityThis three-stage pipeline executes sequentially on bounded edge hardware (e.g., a clustered NVIDIA Orin deployment or an A100-backed MEC node). To quantify the performance of this system accurately, traditional perception metrics such as Intersection over Union (IoU) or mean Average Precision (mAP) are insufficient, as they fail to capture the temporal realities of the autonomous control loop.Instead, the system's efficacy is strictly evaluated using a deadline-qualified, planner-aware utility objective.35 This metric measures the exact value of the perception and prediction outputs at the absolute boundary of the vehicle's local motion planner. In this framework, utility is a strict step-function regarding time: if the predicted trajectory arrives at the vehicle after the planner's rigid execution deadline (e.g., 100 ms after the initial sensor exposure), its utility instantly drops to zero, regardless of its spatial accuracy. If the data arrives on time, its utility score is proportionally weighted by its kinematic relevance to the ego vehicle. For instance, accurately predicting the trajectory of an aggressive, intersecting vehicle generates exponentially higher utility than refining the bounding box coordinates of a stationary vehicle parked 100 meters away.375. Dense-Scale Characterization and the Deadline CliffThe primary phenomenological focus of this research is the characterization of the &quot;deadline cliff.&quot; In isolated, low-density environments (e.g., 4 to 8 agents), the SOTA edge pipeline operates comfortably, executing fusion, tracking, and prediction well within the RTOS temporal constraints. However, as the system scales to dense participation levels characteristic of urban congestion (25 to 50+ agents), it experiences a severe, non-linear operational collapse.5.1 The Joint Pipeline Failure BoundaryA fundamental assertion of this study is that the computational failure boundary is induced by the synergistic weight of the joint pipeline, rather than the catastrophic failure of any single, isolated module. The sequential nature of the pipeline dictates that intermediate fusion outputs must feed the SSM tracker, which in turn must supply localized track histories to the multimodal predictor.As the number of participating agents $N$ increases, the computational complexity scales aggressively across all fronts. The cross-agent spatial attention in the fusion stage scales at $O(N^2)$. Simultaneously, the number of detected objects $M$ in the scene increases as a function of $N$. While the continuous-time SSM tracker maintains an efficient $O(M)$ or $O(M \log M)$ complexity for state propagation, the downstream multimodal predictor must evaluate the geometric interactions between all $M$ tracks, scaling at $O(M^2)$.When mapped to the finite arithmetic logic units (ALUs) and tensor cores of bounded edge hardware, the total compute time per frame, $T_{compute}$, expands geometrically. Eventually, $T_{compute}$ surpasses the physical inter-frame interval of the sensor network (e.g., 100 ms for a standard 10 Hz system).5.2 Cross-Stage Queueing and Age of InformationWhen $T_{compute}$ exceeds the 100 ms threshold, the edge system transitions from a steady state into a severely congested regime. This dynamic is best modeled through standard queueing theory. As the deterministic arrival rate of incoming feature maps ($\lambda$) begins to surpass the degrading service rate of the edge server ($\mu$), the utilization factor $\rho = \lambda/\mu$ approaches 1. Consequently, the queue depth and the wait time ($Q_{delay}$) approach infinity.This queueing pressure causes a rapid and dangerous divergence between the publish-time state (the exact millisecond the vehicle recorded the sensor data) and the use-time state (the millisecond the vehicle's local planner receives the processed prediction). This divergence is quantified as the Age of Information (AoI). Because autonomous local planners execute highly sensitive control commands based on precise state estimates, a sudden spike in AoI renders the provided world model dangerously obsolete.31The transition over the deadline cliff is notably steep. Empirical sweeps indicate that at 24 agents, the pipeline may process end-to-end within 95 ms, maintaining a safe AoI. However, at merely 28 agents, $T_{compute}$ may require 105 ms. Because $\lambda$ remains constant at 10 Hz, a 5 ms processing deficit per frame accumulates rapidly. Within a few seconds of operation, the queue backlog drives the AoI to 500 ms or beyond. At this latency, the predicted trajectories passed to the vehicles are actively harmful, leading to total closed-loop planner failure, forcing emergency safety interventions or resulting in physical collisions.6. Runtime Resource-Aware Adaptation ControllerTo circumvent the deadline cliff and maintain system viability under dense participation, the edge server must abandon rigid, static processing architectures. The proposed solution is a dynamic, runtime resource-aware controller designed to actively orchestrate the edge compute load. By intelligently degrading non-critical dimensions of the workload, the controller maintains pipeline execution strictly within the planner-feasible latency region while simultaneously maximizing safety-relevant utility.6.1 Joint Tri-Factor Adaptation MechanismsPrior attempts at edge orchestration often relied on simplistic, single-variable degradation schemes—such as indiscriminately dropping frames when the CPU saturated. The proposed controller operates over three independent, highly granular optimization variables, offering a mathematically robust approach to load shedding 9:1. Service Update Rate ($r$): The controller possesses the authority to throttle the global processing frequency of the pipeline. Instead of fusing maps and generating predictions at the standard 10 Hz, the controller can dynamically scale the rate down to 5 Hz or even 2 Hz during peak network or compute congestion. While throttling the rate immediately relieves $Q_{delay}$ pressure by reducing $\lambda$, it inevitably increases the temporal uncertainty within the SSM tracking module, forcing the planner to rely on older, albeit highly precise, forecasts between updates.2. Model Fidelity ($f$): The controller can dynamically alter the spatial and computational resolution of the deep learning models at runtime. Within the intermediate fusion perception stage, it can increase the physical voxel size represented in the BEV grid, reducing the tensor dimensions at the cost of fine-grained spatial accuracy. Furthermore, it can actively prune less critical attention heads within the multi-agent fusion mechanism, bypassing vast swaths of matrix multiplication.29 In the multimodal trajectory prediction stage, the controller can reduce the number of generated trajectory modes (scaling $K$ down from 6 to 2) or linearly shorten the forward prediction horizon $T_h$, drastically cutting the $O(M^2)$ interaction modeling costs.3. Model Tier ($m$): To handle extreme spikes in participation, the edge server is provisioned with an ensemble of heterogeneous models. This ensemble ranges from heavily parameterized, high-accuracy foundation models (Tier 1) to aggressively distilled, highly quantized Small Vision Models (SVMs) (Tier 3).39 Under severe compute saturation, the controller can execute a hot-swap, migrating inference workloads from the massive Tier 1 model down to a lightweight Tier 3 variant. This adaptation sacrifices a degree of absolute spatial and kinematic accuracy in exchange for lightning-fast, highly deterministic execution times that guarantee deadline adherence.6.2 Planner-Aware Optimization FormulationThe cognitive logic of the resource-aware controller is fundamentally anchored by the planner-aware utility metric.36 Standard dynamic neural network systems attempt to maximize a generic accuracy metric (like mAP) subject to a hard latency constraint. This approach is fatally flawed for autonomous driving; uniformly dropping 5% accuracy across an entire scene fails to recognize that reducing tracking precision on distant, parked background vehicles is acceptable, whereas reducing precision on an intersecting, high-speed vehicle is a critical safety failure.Let $U_i(r, f, m)$ be the calculated planner-relevant utility for agent $i$ given a specific configuration of rate, fidelity, and tier. Let $C(r, f, m)$ represent the measured computational cost (in milliseconds) required to execute that configuration. At each operational epoch, the controller solves the following maximization problem:$$\max_{r, f, m} \sum_{i \in \text{Agents}} U_i(r, f, m)$$$$\text{subject to: } C(r, f, m) + Q_{delay} \le T_{deadline}$$If the backlog $Q_{delay}$ begins to rise, indicating an impending breach of the $T_{deadline}$ boundary, the controller's optimization algorithm will aggressively degrade fidelity $f$ and model tier $m$ specifically for processing paths associated with low-utility agents. By intelligently shedding compute load from non-critical environmental background elements, the controller ensures that the available tensor cores are strictly reserved for generating high-fidelity, safety-critical predictions. This dynamic tri-factor tuning is what allows the system to preserve high closed-loop success rates even under massive systemic load.7. ConclusionThe inevitable deployment of connected autonomous vehicles in densely populated urban corridors requires a fundamental re-evaluation of both cooperative networking architectures and the computational pipelines that process their data. As rigorously established, decentralized V2V paradigms—relying on 802.11p or PC5 sidelinks—are highly effective for isolated, low-density scenarios but collapse under the physical constraints of channel bandwidth, MAC layer backoff inefficiencies, and accumulative multi-hop jitter when scaled to dense intersections.The necessary transition to a centralized, edge-hosted Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) architecture leveraging 5G/6G V2N2V links solves the networking bandwidth bottleneck, but it inadvertently exposes a critical vulnerability in the compute layer. A joint SOTA pipeline comprising intermediate fusion, SSM-based continuous tracking, and complex multimodal prediction triggers a severe computational deadline cliff under dense participation. The resulting cross-stage queueing drives the Age of Information (AoI) far beyond planner-feasible thresholds, resulting in catastrophic closed-loop failures.The integration of a runtime resource-aware controller provides a robust, highly scalable solution. By dynamically and jointly adapting service rates, model fidelity parameters, and architectural model tiers, the edge orchestrator can actively manage queue depths. Crucially, by steering these adaptations via a planner-aware utility metric, the system ensures that performance degradation occurs strictly on computationally heavy, low-relevance background data, meticulously preserving the fidelity of safety-critical predictions.
